The Line Down the Middle

Two weeks before the spring piano recital, Lily stopped being able to eat breakfast.

It wasn't that she didn't know her piece. She'd been practicing "Spring Waltz" since January, and on a good day she could play it all the way through without looking at the music. The problem was everything else.

At night, lying in bed, she pictured the rows of folding chairs in the community room. She pictured all the parents holding up their phones. She pictured herself missing the high note in the middle section, and someone in the front row (probably Chloe's mom, who always sat in the front row) whispering, "Oh no."

Then she pictured it again. And again.

"You're quiet," said her big brother Ryan on Saturday, flopping onto the couch next to her. Ryan was fifteen, played volleyball, and somehow always knew when something was wrong, even when he was pretending to look at his phone.

"Recital," Lily mumbled.

"Ah. What's the worst part?"

Lily thought about it. "Everybody watching. What if I mess up and they think I'm bad? What if they clap louder for the other kids? What if someone laughs?"

Ryan didn't say "Don't worry" or "You'll be great." Lily was glad, because everyone said that and it never helped. Instead, he grabbed a sheet of paper and drew a line straight down the middle. On the left he wrote MY JOB. On the right he wrote THEIR JOB.

"In volleyball, there's a net," Ryan said. "My job is everything on my side: watching the ball, moving my feet, hitting it over. What happens on the other side, like whether they dive or miss or whether their fans cheer, I can't control. If I spend the game staring at the other side, I miss the ball coming right at me."

Lily frowned. "So?"

"So let's sort your worries. Which side does each one go on?"

They started. Practicing the hard part in the middle section? Lily's side. Getting a good night's sleep before the recital? Lily's side. Walking up to the piano, taking a breath and playing her best? Lily's side.

What the audience thought of her playing? Lily hesitated, pen in the air. She couldn't climb into anyone's head and change their thoughts. "Their side," she said slowly.

Whether someone whispered: their side. Whether other kids got louder clapping: their side. Whether Chloe's mom said "Oh no": their side.

Ryan snorted. "Chloe's mom is probably busy worrying about Chloe."

When they finished, Lily stared at the paper. The THEIR JOB side was full. The MY JOB side had four things on it.

"Huh," she said. "I've been doing the other team's job."

"And that's exhausting. No wonder you can't eat breakfast." Ryan folded the paper in half so only MY JOB showed. "Here. That's your whole job. Four things."

The worry didn't magically disappear over the next two weeks. But now, when the picture of whispering parents popped into her head, Lily had a question to ask: Is this mine to control?

Usually the answer was no. "Not my job," she'd whisper, and go back to practicing the high note.

On recital day, her hands were cold and her heart thumped like a drum. She sat in the second row with the folded paper in her pocket and watched a boy named Marcus forget the middle of his song. He stopped, started again and finished. Everyone clapped. Nobody laughed.

Lily realized she hadn't thought he's bad for even one second. She'd thought, That must have been scary. Good for him.

Then it was her turn.

Walking to the piano felt like walking through Jell-O. She sat down, put her hands on the keys, and felt the old worries crowding in: Everyone's watching. What if—

She touched the paper in her pocket. My job: breathe and play. Their job: everything else.

She breathed. She played.

She missed one note in the middle section, not even the high note, a different one, and her stomach dropped. But she kept going, because keeping going was on her side of the line.

When she finished, the room clapped. She couldn't tell whether it was louder or softer than for anyone else, and she realized she didn't need to know. That wasn't her job.

Afterward, Ryan found her by the cookie table. "Well?"

Lily grinned and held up the folded paper. "I did my job."
